Veterinary neuronavigation: what it is and how image-guided surgery works

BMT Team · August 17, 2026 · 10 min read

Technology transforms the patient's CT and MRI scans into a three-dimensional map tracked in real-time during the procedure. Understand what it does, what it doesn't do, why it has barely reached veterinary medicine, and what changes with a system developed in Brazil.

There is a question every surgeon has silently asked themselves in the middle of a craniotomy: I've opened, now where do I go? Knowing there is a tumor on the scan is one thing; knowing where it is in relation to the tip of the instrument, with the field open and the anatomy shifted, is quite another — and 1 mm separates two different approaches. Neuronavigation exists to answer exactly this: it is an image-guided surgery technology that correlates pre-operative scans with the patient on the table, showing in real-time where the instrument is inside the 3D reconstructed anatomy. In practice, it works like a surgical GPS — only the map is the patient themselves.

In human medicine, this resource has been standard for decades. In veterinary medicine, it has barely arrived, and the reason is rarely clinical: it is the price. The following sections first explain how the technology works in any navigation system — and only then what is specific to Orion One, the first neuronavigator developed for veterinarians, built by BMT.

How neuronavigation works

What is veterinary neuronavigation

Veterinary neuronavigation is a surgical navigation system that shows the position of the instrument in relation to the patient's anatomy and the planned target. The software reconstructs this anatomy from imaging studies, the surgeon defines the target and trajectory before operating, and during the procedure, the screen displays the spatial relationship between the instrument, the trajectory, and the surrounding structures.

The GPS analogy is useful, but it stops at an important point: the neuronavigator does not conduct the surgery. There is no automation of the surgical gesture or decision made by the software. It is an extra layer of spatial information added to the surgeon's training, experience, and direct visualization.

From the scan to the operating room, in four steps

  1. Imaging. Everything starts with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). CT uses X-rays, defines bone structures well, and provides the rigid references that support navigation; MRI, based on magnetic fields and radio waves, characterizes nervous tissue and the extent of a lesion much better. The quality of these studies is the first limit of everything that follows: no system creates information that the scan does not contain.
  2. Reconstruction and planning. The two-dimensional slices are processed into a three-dimensional model of the anatomy — skull, brain, spine, vertebrae. It is on this model that the surgeon studies the approach, marks the target, and plots the trajectory before any incision. This is 3D surgical planning.
  3. Registration. The most critical step. There are two separate spaces — the virtual model and the real patient positioned on the table — and the system needs to establish the correspondence between them. The principle is like comparing two photographs of the same place taken from different angles: you can only overlap them by finding corresponding points. The accuracy of the rest of the procedure depends directly on the quality of this registration.
  4. Tracking. The system still needs to know where the instrument is. This is done by intraoperative tracking, which can be optical — a camera tracks markers attached to the tools — or electromagnetic, among other methods. Once registration is done and the instrument is tracked, the anatomy, target, trajectory, and tool co-exist in the same coordinate system, and this is what appears on the screen during surgery.

Where navigation makes a difference — and where it doesn't

Neuronavigation pays off most exactly where three-dimensional localization is the bottleneck: deep lesions, small targets, complex anatomy, or cases where the approach requires a specific trajectory. Biopsies and approaches to intracranial masses in dogs and cats are among the applications already investigated in veterinary literature.

There are limits that must be considered before adopting any system. A low-quality scan, a shift in patient positioning, and poorly executed registration compromise the entire navigation. And there is brain shift: during a craniotomy, cerebrospinal fluid loss and tissue manipulation shift structures relative to the position recorded in the preoperative scan. Any system based on preoperative images carries this limitation — navigation is an orientation tool, not an automatic guarantee of results.

Why the technology barely reached veterinary medicine

A conventional neuronavigator is expensive for three reasons that have little to do with the software. The first is the camera: traditional tracking relies on an imported infrared module, which is expensive and purchased from third parties. The second is the age of the technology — a good part of the systems in use run on an architecture conceived two or three decades ago. The third is the market, historically concentrated among a few international suppliers, with all the import, exchange rate, and technical assistance infrastructure that this entails.

The result is well-known: equipment that is inaccessible to most clinics and practically absent from veterinary routine. The bottleneck, it must be repeated, is cost — not medicine.

What Orion One does differently

A camera developed from scratch

Instead of outsourcing the imported infrared tracking module that makes traditional systems so expensive, BMT developed its own camera: the Stereo4D, which combines stereo vision and computer vision to calculate the position and orientation of tools in real-time. The principle is the same optical tracking described above — the camera sees markers attached to the tools — and the difference lies in who designs the camera. With the camera and software developed in-house, the cost of navigation is no longer dictated by an import chain. It is this engineering decision — and not a commercial discount — that makes it viable to bring neuronavigation to the veterinary routine.

The system works with 1 mm accuracy. As in any navigation, the effective value in each surgery depends on the quality of the scan, the registration, and the anatomical shifts during the procedure.

Image fusion and tumor segmentation

Orion has an automatic image fusion function: it overlaps MRI and CT in the same space, so the surgeon sees the tumor with MRI definition and the bone with CT detail, without needing to switch between studies.

On top of this fused volume comes tumor segmentation: the lesion is delimited in 3D, giving the surgeon the volume and contours of the target — not just a point on a slice. Planning is then done against the real shape of the mass.

What goes into the room: laptop, camera, and tracked tools

It's worth clearing up a common mental image: with Orion, neuronavigation does not require a fixed tower or construction work in the operating room. The system consists of a laptop with BMT's software and the Stereo4D camera on a tripod — two items that come out of a hard case and are set up next to the table. Those who operate at more than one location can transport everything together with the rest of their equipment.

What is tracked depends on the procedure. For cranial surgery, there are tracked tools like the OrionPen and the bite clip, which serves as a fixed reference attached to the patient's dental arch. For spinal surgery, the OrionPencil and the spinal clip, attached to the vertebra of interest. It is these markers that the camera sees and that tie the virtual model to the real patient.

Modules: one system, multiple specialties

This is a point specific to Orion and not a characteristic of neuronavigation in general. Conventional systems are usually dedicated to a single application — as a rule, the skull. Orion was built in modules that share the same camera, laptop, and software, changing only the planning workflow and the tracked tools:

  • Brain (available) — cranial neurosurgery: intracranial masses, planned craniotomies.
  • Spine (available) — spinal surgery, with its own clip and pointer.
  • Hip (in development) — hip procedures.
  • Biopsy (in development) — guided biopsies.

In practice, this means that the investment is not tied to a single specialty: the clinic that adopts navigation for neurosurgery can extend its use to other fronts without changing equipment.

Equipment placement or pay-per-use: how the clinic pays for it

Reducing manufacturing costs solves half the problem; the other half is the access model. For clinics and hospitals, equipment placement — a free-of-charge loan of the system under contract, known in Brazil as comodato — provides access to Orion without tying up capital in a single piece of equipment, breaking down the main barrier for those wanting to raise their surgical standards without compromising cash flow. For the mobile veterinarian who works at several locations and for whom maintaining fixed equipment makes no sense, the hard-case configuration and the pay-per-use model allow paying for usage rather than acquisition.

In both cases, a third factor is as decisive as price: support and monitoring during procedures. Surgical technology is only worth what it delivers within the reality of the OR — and the initial learning curve is exactly where a system is either adopted or abandoned.

Who develops it

Frequently Asked Questions

About the technology

What is veterinary neuronavigation?
It is an image-guided surgery technology that correlates the patient's scans to their real anatomy during surgery and allows tracking the position of instruments in relation to the reconstructed 3D model.

Does neuronavigation replace the surgeon?
No. It is an orientation tool. Planning, interpretation of information, and clinical decisions remain the surgeon's responsibility.

Which imaging studies are used?
Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. CT provides the bone reference; MRI characterizes the lesion and nervous tissue. Image quality is crucial for navigation to work properly.

Is it suitable for dogs and cats?
Yes, including neurological procedures in small animals. The concrete indication depends on the case, the available scan, and the surgeon's evaluation.

About Orion One

What is Orion's accuracy?
The system operates with an accuracy of 1 mm. The effective value in each surgery depends on the quality of the scan, patient registration, and anatomical shifts that occur during the procedure.

Is Orion only for skull surgery?
No. The system is modular: today Orion already operates with the Brain and Spine modules, but soon we will have the Hip and Biopsy modules, all running on the same camera and software.

Does Orion use MRI or CT?
Both, fused into the same model. MRI and CT fusion allows seeing the lesion and the bone reference in a single three-dimensional study.

What do you need in the room?
Space to position a laptop and the Stereo4D camera on a tripod about 1 meter from the patient, plus the sterilized tracked tools for the procedure. No fixed installation or OR renovation is necessary.

Do I need to buy a neuronavigator?
No. BMT offers equipment placement (comodato) for clinics and a pay-per-use model aimed at professionals who work in different locations. The goal is to reduce the cost of accessing the technology and allow more veterinary surgeons to adopt navigation in their routine.
