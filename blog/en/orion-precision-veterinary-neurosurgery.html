How neuronavigation raises precision in veterinary neurosurgery

BMT · August 15, 2026 · 6 min read

Every neurosurgery is an exercise in precision in a space where millimeters separate safe access from risk. In human medicine, the answer to that problem has had a name for decades: neuronavigation — guiding the instrument in real time over the patient's own imaging. In veterinary practice, that same technology is only now reaching the operating room. That is the story behind Orion One.

From a research project to a product

Orion One was not built from scratch. It is the direct heir of Project Yara, six years of research into robotic assistance for neurosurgery (SEEG) carried out at EESC USP in partnership with the HC of Ribeirão Preto. When four mechatronics engineers founded BMT, the challenge was to translate all of that knowledge into a different reality: the routine — and the budget — of a veterinary clinic.

The question that guided the project was simple: how do you bring hospital-grade precision to the veterinary surgeon without requiring the infrastructure of a large center?

What changes when you navigate in real time

Without navigation, the surgeon plans on the CT or MRI and then mentally reconstructs that map inside the surgical field. With Orion One, the map is no longer mental: the instrument's position appears overlaid on the patient's imaging, updated with every movement.

Navigation does not replace the surgeon's hand — it gives the surgeon back the information the anatomy hides.

In practice, this translates into three concrete gains:

  • A planned, verified trajectory: the approach is defined on the image and checked before and during access.
  • Less unnecessary exposure: a well-located target means more contained incisions and manipulation.
  • Confidence in the decision: the surgeon operates with an objective reference, not just an estimate.

Precision that grows with the clinic

Orion One started with neurosurgery (Orion Brain) and moved on to the spine (Orion Spine), with navigation for implants and pedicle screws. The same technological base supports the next step — hip and new specialties — and connects with the rest of the BMT ecosystem: image capture from Zenith and the clinical cloud platform of Guide.

Precision, in the end, is not an isolated feature. It is a standard of work — and that is what BMT is building, one procedure at a time.
